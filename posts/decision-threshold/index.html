A Steerable Decision Threshold in a Planning Agent

2026-08-28

Summary

Our agents solve mazes with two rewarded objectives and a step cost. The optimal solution involves weighing up the objectives’ rewards against the distances required to reach them. The maximum number of extra steps that the agent will accept to reach the higher valued objective is the decision threshold.

We found that this threshold is linearly encoded in a subset of the model weights. Adjusting the weights by hand moves the agent’s decision boundary as precisely and reliably as fine-tuning with a different reward function. This result was reproduced across DRC (a recurrent planning architecture), ResNet and ViT agents trained with RL, and in a prefix transformer trained by imitation.

Motivation

AI systems increasingly display goal-directed behaviour ranging from the simple “win the game” objective of a chess engine to the more complicated objectives of an LLM (Everitt et al., 2025).

Work on understanding of goal-directed behaviour in agents has so far been largely behavioural (Dennett, 1981; Orseau et al., 2018; MacDermott et al., 2024). However, there are proven limits on what behavioural data alone can tell us about how an agent will act in new, out-of-distribution situations (Bellot et al., 2025).

Many AI safety relevant questions are of this out-of-distribution sort. Why do some models pursue goals more persistently than others? And what proxy goals form during training at all: some theory predicts that optimal policies tend to seek power (Turner et al., 2021) but whether trained agents actually internalise such instrumental goals is unknown.

Mechanistic claims about goals can only be validated in environments with a known ground-truth objective. So our starting point is to build such an environment and attempt to fully reverse-engineer the goal representation our agents learn.

Background

What does it mean for a system to be goal-directed? The classic behavioural definition (Dennett, 1981, later formalised by Orseau et al., 2018 and MacDermott et al., 2024) is that a system is goal-directed to the extent that modelling it as rationally pursuing a goal predicts its behaviour better than any comparably simple goal-free description. This is a matter of degree rather than a binary property and it is a claim about our description of the system as opposed to its internals. But when our goal-model is effective, something inside the agent is making this true. These internal mechanisms are what we aim to understand.

What work has been done in this area so far? In a maze agent known to exhibit goal misgeneralisation, Mini et al., 2023 found the goal representation in the activations and could steer the agent by intervening on it. What they found was not a single clean variable: the representation was spread across many circuits, mixing target-tracking with a simple location bias. And because the training environment left the goal underspecified, there was no quantitative ground truth to validate a goal-reading against.

Planning is the machinery by which an agent might pursue a goal; the goal is the criterion that decides which plan is worth executing. This planning side of the picture is now mapped in some detail. Bush et al., 2025 gave the first mechanistic evidence of emergent planning in a DRC agent (Guez et al., 2019) playing Sokoban, finding internal plans that causally steer its behaviour, and Taufeeque et al., 2024, 2026 traced how these plans are stored: in dedicated channels of the hidden state, readable up to ~50 steps ahead. Similar learned look-ahead has been found in a chess policy network (Jenner et al., 2024).

The Task

We build on Bush et al., 2025, keeping the DRC agent but swapping Sokoban for maze navigation with two rewarded objectives. A single-objective task mostly exercises planning: every candidate plan leads to the same target, and ranking them is simply picking the shortest path. With two objectives and a step cost, plans to different targets must be compared and that comparison is exactly the valuation step we set out to isolate. The underlying maze-navigation skill is unchanged.

Let \(R_A\) and \(R_B\) be the two objectives’ rewards, \(d_A\) and \(d_B\) the path lengths to each, and \(c\) the per-step cost. The optimal policy takes objective A whenever

\[R_{A} - R_{B} > c \, (d_{A} - d_{B})\]

Each maze presents the agent with a distance gap \(\Delta d = d_A - d_B\), and the optimal rule is to accept up to \(\tau^* = (R_A - R_B)/c\) extra steps to reach the higher-reward objective. \(\tau^*\) is the environment’s ground-truth decision threshold. Crucially, the agent never observes the rewards: its input contains only the maze geometry. Whatever threshold \(\hat{\tau}\) it actually uses must be internalised during training.

The mazes are 11x11 perfect mazes so a unique path connects any two cells, and \(d_A\) and \(d_B\) are well-defined. The agent observes a 4×11×11 array: one channel each for the walls, its own position, and the two objectives’ positions (Figure 2a). Walls, start position and objective positions are randomised across levels. Further environment details are in Appendix A

We train four agents on three seeds: a DRC, a ResNet and a ViT with reinforcement learning, plus a prefix transformer behaviour-cloned from optimal solutions. Hyperparameters and training details are in Appendix B.

We reproduced Bush et al.’s planning results on our new environment. We can read the agent’s plan from its activations (Figure 2b) and writing plans in also works, though less reliably than in their Sokoban setting. Nothing downstream depends on plan writing.

Finding the Decision Threshold

From each base agent we fine-tune a grid of arms: copies of the agent trained for a further 400k steps with one goal’s value shifted. The grid covers offsets of ±0.1 to ±0.4 in steps of 0.1 plus a null arm at the base value, for each goal separately, giving 18 arms per base agent (Appendix B). An arm differs from its base only in what the objectives pay.

Each arm leaves a weight change \(\Delta\theta_i = \theta_i - \theta_{\text{base}}\). We fit these jointly with an affine model,

\[\Delta\theta_i \approx \mathbf{d} + \alpha_i\,\hat{\mathbf{v}},\]

where \(\mathbf{d}\) is a drift shared by every arm (the common effect of further fine-tuning, whatever the values), \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\) is a single unit direction, and \(\alpha_i\) is the arm’s value offset. The fit is least squares over the grid arms; extension arms past the grid are held out of every fit.

Decomposing a single arm’s weight change at \(|\Delta v| = 0.40\) into these parts:

Ranges are min–max over independent base agents, held out, both sweeps pooled. † truncated at zero: the raw held-out estimates are negative (ResNet −25.4 [−30.2, −20.2], ViT −19.5); the axis component of a single arm’s weight change is below the fine-tuning noise floor, not measured to be absent.

In weight space, then, a single arm is mostly noise: 64–79% fine-tuning residual in the RL agents, plus a shared drift, with the value-carrying axis a few percent at most. The test of whether that small component is real is writing it back. For each grid offset we take the axis fitted with that arm held out and add it to the frozen base weights, \(\theta + \Delta v\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}\), with no drift term. The write reproduces the held-out arm’s threshold to 0.4 [0.3, 0.5] steps in the DRC, 0.5 [0.2, 0.8] in the ResNet, 0.2 in the ViT and 1.1 [0.9, 1.1] in the BC transformer: a direction at or below the noise floor of any single arm carries essentially the whole behavioural effect. How \(\hat{\tau}\) is estimated from behaviour is described in Appendix 1.

Figure 3 shows both routes to every threshold: fine-tuning at a shifted value, and writing the leave-one-out axis into the frozen base. Within the fitted window the two are near-indistinguishable in every panel.

The manipulation is also surgical. Across the written range the DRC still reaches a goal in 100% of held-out mazes at every dose, within 0.03 steps of the shortest path on average (the data behind Figure 1): the write moves the preference and nothing else we can measure.

Outside the fitted window the axis extrapolates well, until the manipulation asks for a preference reversal: fine-tuning can drive an agent through \(\tau = 0\), and the written axis cannot follow it there, flattening near indifference instead (squares in Figure 3).

The trained arms also read off each agent’s own exchange rate. The task-optimal slope is ±20 steps per unit of value; the DRC moves ≈13, the ResNet 14–18, the unconverged ViT ≈6, and the BC transformer ≈22, tracking its optimal demonstrator. Trained undiscounted, the RL agents have no discounting to excuse the undershoot: they abandon the richer goal sooner than the reward function warrants.

Appendix 2: Training Runs

Architecture, training recipe, budget, and seed selection for every agent in the post. All reinforcement-learning agents were trained undiscounted (γ = 1), so every model optimises exactly the objective the analysis scores.

The task

One 11×11 maze per episode with two objectives, worth 1.0 and 0.5, each marked by a colour. Reaching one ends the episode and pays its value; every step costs 0.05, and an episode is cut off after 120 steps. The optimal policy therefore takes the 1.0 goal unless it is more than (1.0 − 0.5)/0.05 = 10 steps further away. This threshold is the quantity measured throughout the post.

Observation
Four binary 11×11 planes: walls, agent, colour 0, colour 1. There is no value channel: colour is the only cue to what a goal pays, so the values must be learned as internal constants.
Colour–value link
ρ = 1.0: colour 0 always marks the richer goal.
Levels
Pre-generated pool of 1M mazes (1.00-0.50@1M). Training draws from the train split; evaluation and all measurement from held-out splits.

Reinforcement-learning setup

All three RL architectures share one recipe. Only the network differs, so differences between agents are attributable to architecture rather than training.

Algorithm
IMPALA with V-trace (λ = 0.97), 256 parallel environments, 20-step rollouts, 8 minibatches per update.
Discount
γ = 1.0 (undiscounted): the trained objective is exactly the value-minus-step-cost quantity every measurement uses.
Optimiser
Adam; learning rate annealed 4×10⁻⁴ → 4×10⁻⁶; gradient-norm clip 0.015.
Loss weights
Entropy 0.01, value 0.25.
ViT exception
Peak rate 1×10⁻³ with floor 1×10⁻⁴, chosen by a learning-rate sweep; the inherited schedule left it still improving when the rate ran out.

Value sweeps

Each base agent is fine-tuned onto a grid of altered goal values; the fitted direction through those weight changes is the value axis. Arms differ only in what the objectives pay.

Grid
Offsets of ±0.1 to ±0.4 in steps of 0.1, plus a null arm at the base value, for each of the two goals separately: 9 arms per goal per base.
Per arm
400k steps at a constant 10⁻⁴ (annealing would put each arm at a different point on its own schedule), on a 150k-level dataset generated at that arm’s values.
Fitting
The axis is least-squares fitted from the symmetric grid alone, with the common fine-tuning drift removed. Extension arms are held out of every fit by construction.
Past parity
Arms that take the swept goal beyond the other use levels whose colours are pinned to objectives. Under the usual scheme the generator repaints whichever goal is richer, so such an arm would learn a relabelled preference instead of a reversed one.
Measurement
Every arm and every written model is decoded at the base values on held-out levels, so all thresholds are comparable regardless of what an arm trained on.

A depth series at d = 256 (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 layers, 1.6M–50.6M parameters) and a narrow-deep control (d 128, 32 layers) were trained on the same recipe; the 16-layer configuration has three seeds and the rest one. Axis reliability is the split-half correlation of the fitted direction; cos(v̂₀, v̂₁) is the angle between the directions fitted from sweeping each goal separately, where −1 means the two sweeps move one shared knob rather than two independent value registers.

Two configurations, d256 L8 and L16, sit well below the rest on both measures. Their axes remain present and graded: the fitted direction recovers an arm’s offset with correlation 0.985, as high as any other model, but at roughly half scale (slope 0.46 against 0.90 elsewhere) on the same 13-arm grid. This is the signature of noisier weight changes attenuating the fit, which pulls reliability and the cosine toward zero together, rather than of a value axis that stops existing at those sizes.

The 0.8M model here is architecturally identical to the post’s BC transformer but is not the same run: this family trains on 7.84M demonstrations against the post model’s 900k.

Compute

Everything was trained on single RTX 4090s. A DRC or ResNet base is roughly 4–5 hours, a ViT base around 5.5 hours, and a full 18-arm value sweep with its measurements about 1.5 hours per base. The scaling family’s models train in minutes to a few hours each, being far smaller runs over a fixed demonstration set.
